01Executive summary

The KTP DevOps team is developing Kortex, an internal platform for the KTP Suite — the family of treasury and payments products comprising CTI, KTP, FFC, Payment Factory, KTPWeb and Cargo.

Kortex is not a question-and-answer assistant, and we want that clear from the outset. The intent is an alternative entry point for the team's daily work: open it to see what needs attention today, ask what a product does and get an answer sourced from our own documentation, look up a support case or a vulnerability — and act on it. Some of that acting changes state in downstream systems. Section 7 sets out exactly where, and what we propose to do about it.

The initiative is at an early development stage. A working prototype demonstrates the core pattern end to end; the multi-agent extension is written but has not been validated. Development is currently paused because the model service the prototype was built against — GitHub Models — has been fully retired. Section 4 sets that out in full.

We are presenting now rather than later for three reasons, each of which maps to something the CoE exists to do. We are asking before we build, while the design decisions that are expensive to reverse are still open. We are asking whether this has already been solved at Finastra, particularly on model procurement, where we are stuck on a problem another team has probably already worked through. And we want the security, privacy and regulatory standards designed in from the start rather than retrofitted, which is why Section 7 leads on data protection rather than treating it as an annex.

What we are asking forA sanctioned model service so development can resume; confirmation that the data flows in Section 5 and the protections in Section 7 meet the required standard; and agreement on the readiness gate in Section 9 that must be cleared before anyone outside the pilot team uses this.

We are not asking for production approval, and we are not presenting a finished system. What we are asking the CoE to judge is whether the idea is sound, whether the approach is the right one, and what conditions we should build to.

02Objectives and business value

The KTP DevOps team supports a product family whose knowledge is fragmented across product documentation, Salesforce cases, ServiceNow vulnerability records, Jira issues and the experience held by individual engineers. Four objectives follow from that: reaching product knowledge, reaching resolution knowledge, seeing the day's work in one place, and building the capability to do more of this.

Who this is for

The pilot population is the DevOps and Client Services teams — a mix of technical and client-facing roles, each of which uses the platform differently.

ProfileWhat Kortex does for them
SupportDiagnose issues, find solutions in documentation, work through case resolution
Consultants and project managersImplementation guidance, case follow-up, go-live support
DevOps and SRETechnical ticket triage, vulnerability management, database operations
ManagementStatus summaries, dashboard insight, escalation support

Objective 1 — Product answers without waiting for the person who knows

The KTP Suite is large, and the people who understand it best are the people most interrupted. Finding out how something works today means reading through product documentation, or asking a colleague and waiting — and that colleague may be in another time zone, on leave, or in the middle of their own escalation. The knowledge is not missing. Reaching it depends on someone's availability.

Kortex answers product questions directly from the official documentation, with citations back to the source, and — this matters more than it first sounds — correct for the version the user is actually running. Someone working on CTI 23.2 gets 23.2's answer, not 24.1's. This is also the clearest thing a general-purpose assistant cannot do for us: it has no way of knowing which release is in front of the person asking.

Three consequences follow, and each is worth stating separately:

  • Specialist time is reclaimed. Routine questions stop arriving at the few people who can answer everything.
  • New joiners get further alone. Onboarding currently runs on shoulder-tapping. This is also the benefit easiest to measure, which matters given we have no baseline for anything else.
  • Answers become traceable. Every response cites its source and is recorded. A corridor conversation is neither — so this makes knowledge transfer auditable in a way our current practice is not.

The corpus is maintained operationally: documentation is uploaded as it is published, and on each release. Version history is retained deliberately, because colleagues supporting older releases need the answer that was true for theirs.

Objective 2 — Get from symptom to resolution faster

When an engineer picks up an application error or a vulnerability report, the work divides into two parts: working out what is going on, and fixing it. The second part is often minutes. The first routinely takes days.

It takes days because there is no way to know whether a precedent exists. So the engineer reads the documentation, searches related email threads, and asks colleagues whether anyone has seen it before — completing that whole sweep before establishing whether the answer was already written down by someone else months ago. When it was, the exploration was wasted. When it was not, the exploration was necessary but nobody else benefits from it next time.

The knowledge that would shorten this exists, but not in the official documentation. It is in the investigation and resolution write-ups produced every time somebody solves one of these — and those live in individual reports and individual memories. Objective 1 is about reaching knowledge that has been published. This one is about reaching knowledge we had to earn.

Two kinds of work sit here, and they behave the same way:

  • Application bugs and errors — where a colleague has almost certainly met something similar.
  • Vulnerability handling — where a report arrives, is distributed across the team, and each engineer investigates and remediates for their own case or server. Our accumulated assessments describe how vulnerabilities actually affect our products, which no external source knows.

The question we want answerable in seconds is the one that currently takes days to even ask: have we seen this before, and what did we actually do about it?

Objective 3 — One place to start the day

A working day currently begins by checking several systems in turn to establish what needs attention — Jira for issues, Salesforce for cases, ServiceNow for vulnerability records, the databases for whatever is running. Reconstructing that picture is a daily tax, and it is paid before any actual work starts.

Kortex is intended to present that picture in one place, scoped to the individual: what has moved, what is waiting, what needs a decision — and then let the person act on it without leaving. Salesforce, ServiceNow, Jira, Office 365 and our database administration application are integrated to make this possible, but the integration is the method, not the point. The point is being able to start.

The case-oriented integrations handle client-identifying content and therefore carry a higher classification than the rest of the platform; Section 7 sets out how we propose to treat them.

Objective 4 — Build AI development skills in the team

The team is new to building with AI, and this initiative is deliberately also a learning exercise. The intent is to expand the team's skillset with the technologies this kind of work requires — retrieval, orchestration, evaluation, and the frameworks around them, of which LangChain is one example among several — rather than to acquire a single tool.

The expected return is optionality. A team that understands how these systems are built recognises where else they would help, and can judge what to build, what to buy and what to leave alone. We would equally welcome the CoE pointing us toward teams further ahead than us; learning in that direction is the more valuable one.

On benefit measurementWe do not have a measured baseline for any of these objectives, and at this stage we would not expect to. Figures quoted now would be estimates presented as evidence.

We propose instead that establishing a baseline becomes a condition of proceeding past prototype: across the DevOps and Client Services pilot, instrument how long a defined set of questions takes to answer today against how long it takes through Kortex, over a four-week window. The telemetry needed to do this is already part of the design.

04Where the project stands

We would rather be direct about this than present the initiative as further along than it is.

What has been demonstrated

The core pattern works end to end: Entra sign-in, role-based access, semantic retrieval over an ingested corpus, streamed answers with source citations, and conversation memory. User-selectable models worked as well — a user could choose the model suited to the task, and the interface adapted to what that model supported. The multi-agent extension — orchestrator, CVE agent, database agent — is written and structurally complete, but has not been validated against real questions or real users.

It is worth being clear that the withdrawal did not simply pause new work. It disabled something that was working. Model selection is not unbuilt; it is unusable, because there is no longer a catalogue to select from. It returns the day there is an approved one.

What has stopped

Development is blockedKortex was built against GitHub Models for both chat inference and embeddings. That service has now been fully retired. Every path through the system depends on it — answering, intent classification, document grading and corpus embedding — so development has halted rather than slowed.

This is the single reason we are bringing the submission now rather than after further build.

In fairness to the decision that has been made for us: GitHub Models was an experimentation-tier service, and the prototype had already run into its limits. Our own status report records first responses taking around a minute because of rate-limit backoff, and the ingestion pipeline carries a deliberate pause between embedding batches for the same reason. We would have had to move to a production-grade inference service regardless. Its retirement has brought that decision forward rather than created it.

Why this is a procurement question, not an engineering one

The model client is built against an OpenAI-compatible interface with a configurable endpoint. Any provider offering that interface is a configuration change — endpoint, credential, model name — not a redesign. The architecture in Section 5 is unaffected by which provider is chosen.

There is one real migration cost. The embedding model determines the vector dimensions of the corpus index, so changing embedding provider means re-embedding the entire corpus and recreating the index. That is manageable now, while the corpus is small, and progressively less so later. It also argues for choosing an embedding model we can commit to for some years.

What this now costs

Worth stating plainly, because it changes the business case. The prototype ran on a service that carried no marginal cost to us. Every replacement above has one: managed services charge per token, and self-hosting substitutes GPU capacity and operational ownership for that. This initiative no longer carries zero marginal cost, and we are not going to present it as though it does.

We are not putting a figure in this paper, because a credible one depends on which provider is chosen and on usage volumes we have not yet measured. We will produce an estimate once D-1 is settled, alongside the four-week baseline described in Section 2.

Two aspects of the design make the cost shape more predictable than it might first appear. The corpus is embedded once and incrementally thereafter — with content-hash deduplication preventing repeat work — rather than per query. And the lightweight steps in each request, intent classification and document grading, are already routed to a smaller and cheaper model than the one that composes the answer. Both were design decisions taken for latency and are equally useful for cost.

05Solution architecture

Kortex is a three-tier containerised application. A web front end handles sign-in and proxies requests server-side. A back end validates the token, enforces role-based access, and passes the request to an orchestrator that classifies intent and routes to the appropriate agent. All state — vectors, conversation memory, user records — is held in self-hosted stores.

For this review, the material question is not the tier diagram but the trust boundary: what leaves Finastra-controlled infrastructure, and what rides on it.

Request path

  1. The user signs in through the corporate identity provider; the session is held server-side.
  2. A question is posted to the front-end proxy, which attaches the bearer token and forwards to the back end.
  3. The back end validates the token against the provider's published signing keys, resolves the user's role, and checks that the role grants the chat module. Users without an approved role are refused.
  4. The orchestrator classifies the question and extracts any product, version or CVE identifier.
  5. The request is routed to the matching agent. The Search Agent retrieves from the vector store, grades the results for relevance, and falls back to web search only when internal documents do not answer the question.
  6. The answer is streamed back token by token. The conversation turn is written to the cache with a 24-hour expiry, and the interaction is traced for observability.

The client-data boundary, stated precisely

A blanket claim that no client data is in scope would be convenient and would not survive examination. The useful distinction is not whether client-derived data is involved, but whether it comes to rest inside Kortex.

Ingested into the corpusRetrieved for one task
What happens to itSplit into extracts, embedded, indexed, stored indefinitelyFetched on demand for a single request, then discarded
Who can reach it afterwardsAnyone the retrieval layer servesOnly the user whose task it was
Sent to the model providerYes — the full document, at ingestion, and extracts thereafterOnly if it forms part of that request's context
LifetimeUntil explicitly deletedExpires with the conversation — 24 hours

The commitment we propose to hold is about the first column: client data is never ingested into the corpus. That is a meaningful, testable constraint — the corpus is the part of the system that persists, is searchable by everyone it serves, and is sent to the model provider in full at ingestion. Keeping it free of client data is worth committing to and worth enforcing at the administrator review described above.

The second column is a different proposition and needs its own controls rather than a prohibition. As the case, ticket and mailbox integrations move from standalone tools into Kortex agents, client-identifying content will be retrieved through Kortex for specific tasks. That is the point of the platform — a support engineer should be able to ask about the case they are working on. Four controls make it defensible:

ControlWhy
Retrieve using the user's own entitlements in the source system, never a broad service accountOtherwise Kortex becomes a way to see cases a person could not open directly — the same concern raised for database access, in a different system. This is the control that matters most.
Never ingested, never embeddedRetrieved case content is used for the request and discarded. It does not enter the corpus and does not become searchable by others.
Retention follows the conversationChat history expires after 24 hours. Client-derived content inherits that lifetime rather than acquiring its own.
Observability retention must account for itTraces record full prompts and responses. If a case is pulled into a conversation, it appears in that trace — so the retention ruling we are asking for (D-4) governs client-derived content, not only internal questions. This raises its importance.

Two further routes, stated for completeness. Test and troubleshooting databases hold anonymised client data and are reachable through the database operations capability; we are asking the CoE to assess whether that anonymisation is sufficient rather than asserting it ourselves. And case-specific troubleshooting documents, planned for the corpus, may carry client identifiers incidentally — which is precisely what the administrator review must screen for, not only document quality.

No production client environment is reachable by any path.

The flow that carries document content out

One flow carries Finastra's own material beyond our infrastructure: the model inference call, which sends the user's question together with the document extracts retrieved to answer it. Everything that holds our data — the index, chat history, user records — is self-hosted. Web search is a lesser case, dealt with immediately below: it sends composed query terms rather than document content, but it is not nothing and we treat it separately.

That makes the provider decision the most consequential data-protection decision in the initiative, and we propose the following are treated as minimum requirements of any provider:

  • No training, fine-tuning or human review of our inputs and outputs
  • Zero or short, contractually defined retention of prompts and responses
  • A processing region we can specify and evidence
  • Tenant isolation, with private connectivity in preference to the public internet
  • Terms that support the commitments Finastra itself makes to its clients

If the corpus classification turns out to be stricter than we assume, the self-hosted model option in Section 4 removes this flow entirely. That trade — operational cost against zero content egress — is the CoE's to weigh.

Where web searches actually go

Self-hosting the search service removes the vendor account and the query history attached to it. It does not, by itself, stop the query reaching the public internet — so the precise position is worth stating.

The metasearch service holds no index of its own. It forwards a query to whichever upstream engines it is configured for and merges the results. Five are enabled: Google, Bing and DuckDuckGo for general results, Wikipedia for reference, and Stack Overflow for technical questions. Outbound requests route through the corporate proxy, so search egress is subject to the same inspection, logging and policy as any other corporate traffic.

The residual is straightforward and we would rather name it than have it inferred: those five engines receive the query text. It is attributable to our corporate egress, not to an individual or to any account we hold with them.

Keeping client-derived content away from outbound search

That leaves one flow needing an explicit interlock. If a conversation has pulled in case content, and the assistant later falls back to web search, the query it composes could carry something from that case outward. The two capabilities are individually reasonable and problematic in sequence.

We propose to close this deterministically rather than by judgment. Once any integration has returned client-sourced content into a conversation, outbound web search is disabled for the remainder of that conversation — a state flag set when the retrieval happens, not an assessment made afterwards. We deliberately do not want a model deciding whether a given exchange is safe to send outside; a probabilistic classifier is the wrong instrument for a control that must not fail.

Two further measures sit alongside it. Search queries are constructed from extracted parameters — product, version, error code — rather than assembled from free conversation text, so what leaves is bounded by design. And known client identifiers are screened before egress, giving a second check that does not depend on the first.

The effect is that a conversation is either an internal-context conversation or an external-search one, and never silently both.

Controlling access to knowledge, not just to the application

Role-based access governs who may use each part of the platform. It does not, today, govern which documents a user's questions can reach: retrieval queries the whole corpus. That is acceptable while everything in the corpus is readable by the whole team, and it stops being acceptable the moment a restricted document is ingested.

We propose to design entitlement filtering into retrieval now — attaching access metadata at ingestion and filtering by the caller's role at query time — because retrofitting it once a corpus has grown is materially harder. We would welcome a view on whether the CoE considers this mandatory from the outset.

Where Kortex acts, not just answers

Because Kortex is intended as an entry point for the team's work rather than a reference tool, some of what it does changes state in other systems. We would rather set this out ourselves, with the controls we propose, than have it emerge in review.

Two capabilities that change stateDatabase operations — in use today. Kortex calls our existing database administration application through a dedicated service account. Which users may do what is decided inside Kortex: an administrator grants read and/or write privilege, per database.

Remediation on servers — planned, not built. For repetitive vulnerability cases whose fix is well understood, the intent is that Kortex prepares and applies the remediation rather than only reporting it. This requires access to the servers concerned.

These are a categorical step beyond retrieval, and they attract the governance weight of the initiative. The risks are well understood — most map to the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications — and each has a strategy we intend to build to.

RiskWhy it applies hereOur strategy
Prompt injection reaching an action Retrieved document content enters the model's context and influences what it does. With an action capability attached, a document carrying instructions — planted, or simply inherited from a vendor PDF — could in principle steer an operation. No path from retrieved content to a state change without a person in between. Retrieved material is structurally separated from instruction in the prompt. Documents are reviewed before ingestion (below), so the corpus is a controlled input rather than an open one.
Excessive agency An agent granted broad capability can take actions wider than the request warranted, and a language model's choice of action is not deterministic. Read and write are separate capabilities with separate grants. Every state change requires explicit human confirmation showing the exact operation before it runs, with a preview where the downstream interface supports one. Server remediation is limited to a closed, named list of case types — never "cases that look similar."
Attribution Downstream systems see the Kortex service account rather than the person who asked, so their logs alone cannot answer "who did this?" Kortex passes the requesting user's identity on every downstream call, and the database administration application records it alongside the service account. Kortex additionally keeps its own trail: who asked, what was proposed, who approved, what ran, what returned. Attribution end to end.
Privilege escalation into Kortex Holding a privileged downstream credential makes Kortex itself a target. Any authentication or authorisation weakness in it now carries the severity of what it can reach. The authentication and authorisation hardening in Section 8 is treated as a prerequisite for the action capabilities, not as general improvement, and is sequenced first.
Blast radius An automated remediation applied at speed can be applied at scale, and an incorrect one propagates faster than a human would. One target at a time, rate-limited, never fleet-wide. Every action reversible, with the rollback prepared before execution. Credentials scoped to exactly the permitted remediations rather than general administrative rights.
Misinformation acted upon A vulnerability verdict or a proposed fix produced by a model could be wrong, and wrong in a way that reads as confident. No verdict counts as an assessment of record without named human confirmation. Answers cite their sources so the underlying material can be checked rather than trusted.

Quality control on what enters the corpus

Because everything Kortex asserts is grounded in the corpus, what goes into it matters as much as who can query it. Ingested documents pass through administrator review before they are indexed. That review is the control point for three things at once: whether the document is accurate enough to ground answers, whether it carries client or personal data that should not be present, and whether it contains anything that should not be treated as instruction. Upload already requires a specific privilege, and content is deduplicated by hash so the same document cannot be indexed twice.

Accountability for what the system asserts

The CVE agent produces an impacted / not-impacted verdict. We propose that no such verdict counts as an assessment of record without named human confirmation, with that confirmation recorded. We also need to establish whether any output of this pipeline could reach client-facing communications — KTP Suite is software sold to financial institutions, and product vulnerability statements have a way of travelling. If such a path exists, the controls around it should be set by the CoE and not by us.

Regulatory questions we need confirmed

We are flagging these as questions rather than asserting answers.

  • Whether this must be registered in a Finastra AI system inventory, and under what classification.
  • Its classification under the EU AI Act. We would expect an internal productivity assistant to sit well below the high-risk threshold, carrying transparency obligations toward its users rather than the high-risk regime — but that is an expectation, not a determination.
  • Whether a data protection impact assessment is required, given that user-typed questions may incidentally contain personal data even though the corpus does not.
  • Whether ICT third-party risk obligations arising from Finastra's position as a supplier to financial institutions extend to a model provider used in internal tooling.
  • What, if anything, must be disclosed to clients about AI use in the processes that support their products.

Application hardening Security headers on every response, restricted CORS, input sanitisation, rate limiting, non-root containers, API documentation disabled. Make rate limiting per-user rather than per-source-address; validate upload paths; allowlist selectable models.
Audit and observability Structured logging including provisioning and access-request events; metrics; full model-call tracing. Set the trace retention policy (D-4); add an audit record for confirmed CVE verdicts.
Testing and assurance None No automated test suite and no CI security gates. Test the controls we claim, and gate the pipeline on linting, dependency and secret scanning.
Supply chain Partial Our own assessment marks this incomplete. Dependency scanning, software bill of materials, image signing.
Answer quality Unmeasured Nothing evaluates whether answers are correct or properly grounded. Groundedness and citation scoring, plus a fixed retrieval regression set.

On the development exceptionDisabling certificate verification was a deliberate shortcut to get past corporate proxy interception during early development, and the correct fix is recorded alongside it in the code. We are naming it here rather than leaving it to be found: it must not survive into anything users depend on, and it is the first item in the hardening work.

The full engineering task list behind this table — every item, with file references and sequencing — is maintained alongside the codebase and can be provided to the CoE or to a security reviewer on request.

Two observations worth drawing out of this table. Four of the eight reach systems holding client-identifying content, which is why Section 7 treats retrieval entitlements as a design decision rather than a later refinement — an agent reading with a broad service account would let a user see cases they could not open directly. And the Reports agent inherits the classification of whatever it renders: a branded PDF assembled from case data carries that data out of the platform in a file, so its output needs the same handling as its input.
